Images of the Russian Empire: Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection

Aligning three glass plate exposures into one colour photo.

1. Approach

Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire in colour around 1907-1915, long before colour film, by taking three exposures of the same scene one after another through a blue, a green and a red filter. The Library of Congress scanned each glass plate as one tall grayscale image with the three exposures stacked top to bottom in B, G, R order. To get the colour photo back I cut the scan into thirds and align the G and R thirds onto B by translation only, then stack them as R, G, B.

The main idea is to fix a centre box on the reference image (the B channel), then read the same-sized box from the moving image (G or R) at different offsets within a window, and see which offset gives the best score. I score a pair of boxes two ways. SSD is the L2 distance, the square root of the sum of squared pixel differences, where 0 means identical so lower is better. NCC subtracts each box's mean, scales each to unit length and takes the dot product, where 1 means identical so higher is better. NCC is the default because the mean subtraction means it does not care if one channel is brighter overall than another (church is B 0.72 vs R 0.51 on average).

The centre box matters more than the metric. Each channel has a black frame round it that does not move with the picture, and cathedral's top 20 rows average 0.68 against 0.40 for the scene, so if the frame is scored the search lines the frames up instead of the photo. I cut 15% off every side, which keeps 49% of the pixels and clears the worst frame (4.1% on self_portrait) with room to spare. Without it all three JPEGs come out wrong. Reading the box at an offset instead of rolling the whole image also means nothing wraps round, so there is no garbage at the edges to worry about while scoring.

Once the best shift is found I roll the entire raw R and G channels onto B with np.roll and stack them. Rolling wraps the pushed-off edge round to the other side, which shows as a coloured band, so the largest shift per side is trimmed off the final image.

2. Single-scale alignment

For the small JPEGs an exhaustive search is enough: try every (dx, dy) in a +/-15 window, which is 31 x 31 = 961 candidates, and keep the best. Both metrics agree on all three plates and each takes about a second.

3. Multi-scale pyramid

The full-size .tif plates are about 3200 x 3700 per channel and the shifts are ten times bigger, up to 178 px on melons. A window wide enough to reach that is 160,000 candidates, each scored on 12 megapixels, which is hours per image. So for these I use a pyramid: halve both images (with skimage.transform.rescale and anti-aliasing on, since just dropping pixels aliases and the small image stops looking like the big one) until the short side is 200 px or less, then run the +/-15 search there where it is cheap. Walking back up, one pixel at the small size is two at the next, so I double the shift and search only +/-2 round it to fix the rounding. Four halvings of 3200 px gives +/-240 px of reach at full size, and a plate takes about 8-10 seconds. On the three JPEGs the pyramid returns exactly what single-scale did.

With NCC on raw pixels, 13 of the 14 provided plates align correctly. The one failure is the emir, which is discussed in the last two sections. SSD also loses church (R (270, 64)), so NCC is the metric I trust.

5. Bells & Whistles: better features

The emir's robe is a saturated blue, so it is bright in the B exposure and nearly black in R. The raw pixel values of R and B genuinely do not agree at the true shift, and NCC settles on a confidently wrong answer, R (44, 64) instead of (40, 107). Edges do not have this problem: the outline of the robe is in the same place whether the robe is bright or dark. So I run the same pyramid search on each channel's Sobel gradient magnitude (skimage.filters.sobel) instead of its raw pixels. Sobel is a [1, 2, 1] smoothing in one direction times a [-1, 0, 1] difference in the other, so it finds edges without amplifying noise the way a plain difference does. The shift found on edges is still applied to the raw pixels, so only the search changes.

This fixes the emir without touching anything else: on all 17 other plates the edge result is within 2 px of the pixel result. With edges the full pipeline aligns 18 of 18.

6. Failure cases

With the required baseline (pyramid, NCC on raw pixels), 1 of the 18 plates fails: the emir, for the reason above. With SSD it is 2, since church's R channel also goes wrong. With the Sobel edge features from Section 5, 0 of 18 fail.

One custom plate I tried and dropped was a sunset over the sea. NCC put R 15 rows the wrong way and the sun appeared twice. The scene is almost all smooth sky and flat water, and the one strong feature is the horizon, which is a horizontal line and so matches itself at any vertical shift. There was nothing for the metric to lock onto, which is the same kind of failure as the emir with a different cause.
